## Summary

- China enforces total surveillance on its Turkic Muslim population in Xinjiang through the **Integrated Joint Operations Platform**, which is a central big data system that aggregates information from a vast network of human and machine sensors to monitor the region’s 13 million Turkic Muslims
    - The information collected consists of granular personal details, including height down to the centimeter, blood type, political affiliation, and “religious atmosphere” in the home
    - The system uses algorithmic micro-clues to criminalise everyday activities, flagging people for not socialising with neighbors, avoiding the front door, or using more electricity than is considered normal
    - When the system detects an irregularity, it sends an automated investigative mission directly to an officer’s smartphone. If suspicions cannot be immediately eliminated, officials are instructed to place the suspect in “concentrated training” (internment camps)
- The IJOP’s technological regime is reinforced by a strong police force
    - Xinjiang is one of the most heavily policed regions in the world, with twice as many People’s Police per capita than the national average and up to 70,000 Assistant Police
    - Under programs like “fanghuiju” and “Becoming Family”, over 200,000 cadres conduct intrusive home visits to surveil families and input their private data directly into the IJOP app

## Personal reflection

The digital policing of Uyghurs raises socio-political and moral questions regarding the reach of state security and the boundaries of technological control. It is particularly startling how the Chinese state criminalises routine cultural and religious behaviors—such as using unauthorized apps like WhatsApp or simply avoiding one’s front door—as grounds for indefinite detention. This system is underpinned by the Integrated Joint Operations Platform (IJOP), a predictive algorithm that relies on biased police dossiers and manipulated statistics to create a positive feedback loop of repression.

The consequences extend far beyond the 13 million Turkic Muslims in Xinjiang, as these surveillance tools have already been exported to at least 18 other countries. Beyond the immediate human rights impact, this framework allows private firms to treat an entire ethnic group as a laboratory for AI breakthroughs. While the Chinese state is the primary actor, it is disturbing that major Western tech firms like Intel and NVIDIA have reportedly provided the hardware enabling this racial profiling. Ultimately, this sets a dangerous precedent where technology is used to engineer and control reality, entirely bypassing legal due process and democratic scrutiny.

Because the Chinese government’s underlying motivations are fundamentally unjustified, it is futile to propose “better” solutions that align with its current objectives. Instead, the international community must focus on external countermeasures to check the state's behavior. This should include enforcing targeted export controls on Western tech firms engaged in the surveillance trade, introducing stricter regulations on firms implementing stricter global regulations on the design and manufacture of biometric profiling tools, and imposing sanctions on authoritarian regimes that benefit from the global diffusion of China’s surveillance technologies.

## Essay question applicability

1. **How far should a state have a right to monitor the actions of people within its borders?** (2007 A-Levels)
    - How it supports:
    - Limitation / counterpoint: While the Xinjiang case study represents an extreme of state overreach, one could argue from the **Capability** and **Duty** perspectives that a state has a legitimate moral obligation to monitor activity to ensure **National Security**. Proponents might argue that such surveillance is a prerequisite for stability, which is necessary to protect the majority of the population from civil unrest or violence.
2. **To what extent is it acceptable for technology to be used for the purpose of national security?** (Adapted from themes in 2018 EJC and 2020 A-Level Q9)
    - How it supports:
    - Limitation / counterpoint: Using the **Theory vs Practice** heuristic, one could argue that while technology like the **IJOP** aims to provide security in theory, the "algorithmic micro-clues" used to flag individuals for everyday activities (e.g., "avoiding the front door") may lead to high rates of **false positives** in practice. This suggests that the problem lies not with the use of technology itself, but with the **unreliable** nature of the algorithms used to interpret human behaviour.
3. **To what extent should the State be responsible for protecting our privacy in the digital age?** (2019 EJC)
    - How it supports:
    - Limitation / counterpoint: The case study focuses on state-led intrusion, but a counter-argument could consider **Culpability**. It may be argued that in a digital age, individuals and private corporations also bear responsibility for privacy. If citizens voluntarily provide data to platforms that the **IJOP** then aggregates, the duty of care is distributed across multiple stakeholders, rather than resting solely with the state
4. **In the modern world, have we become over-reliant on surveillance to maintain social order?** (2003 A-Levels)
    - How it supports:
    - Limitation / counterpoint: This question addresses the complication of **Excess** (going beyond what is permitted). A counterpoint could be found in **Natural Factors**: the nature of modern security threats—which are often decentralised and digital—may necessitate a level of surveillance that previously seemed excessive but is now a realistic adaptation to modern geopolitical realities. What is labeled as over-reliance might instead be a transition toward national survival in an age of global volatility.
