## Summary

Due to the collapse of education and safety, child recruitment is flourishing. Furthermore, more than three million children in Haiti require humanitarian assistance with no safe place for the children in the city as more than 90% of the capital, Port-au-Prince, is controlled by armed groups. In addition, the children are also unable to go to school as it is not safe outside for them without risking a crossfire. Moreover, the child recruitment is from 30 to 50 percent with child recruitment skyrocketing by 200% in 2025. The children are forced to fight, exploited and robbed of their future. Some children are even used as shields in clashes with the police.

With Haiti being one of the world’s top five hunger spots, child recruitment flourishes as the children are hungry, out of school, displaced or separated from their caregivers. Thus, many children are recruited with the promise of the pair of sneakers, $100 and the certainty of a reliable meal. This is similar to the 2010 Haiti Earthquake which displaced the families eight, nine, ten times in that period with a series of crisis with no recovery between them. Hence, many of the armed group leaders were children in 2010, where their education disrupted, communities destroyed and their futures never rebuilt.

## Essay question applicability

1. **"How effective are international efforts to ease the problem of global hunger?" (2010)**
* **How it supports:** Haiti is currently one of the world's top five hunger spots. This case study illustrates how hunger is a primary driver for child recruitment, as children are lured into armed groups with the "certainty of a reliable meal." This highlights that international efforts fail to protect children from exploitation if they cannot first secure basic food stability.
* **Limitation/counterpoint:** Using the **Symptom vs. Root Cause** framework, one could argue that international aid focusing solely on food distribution only treats the symptom. The root cause—gang control over 90% of the capital—prevents food from reaching those in need, making aid ineffective unless the security vacuum is addressed first.


2. **"Foreign aid does not solve long-term problems." To what extent is this a fair viewpoint? (2018)**
* **How it supports:** The case study highlights a cycle of crisis starting from the 2010 Haiti Earthquake, which displaced families multiple times with no recovery between events. Many current armed group leaders were children during that period whose futures were never rebuilt, suggesting that past aid provided only temporary relief without achieving structural stability.
* **Limitation/counterpoint:** A counter-argument can be made via the **Capability heuristic**. The failure to solve long-term problems may not be a failure of the "aid" itself, but a lack of local institutional capability to absorb and utilize that aid for reconstruction, especially when successive crises occur with no "recovery window" in between.


3. **"Countries experiencing conflict should be left to sort out their own problems." How far do you agree? (2016)**
* **How it supports:** With over 90% of the capital under the control of armed groups and the total collapse of education and safety, the state lacks the capability to protect its own citizens. This presents a moral argument for international intervention to ensure the "freedom from fear" for over three million children.
* **Limitation/counterpoint:** From the perspective of **Natural Factors**, external intervention might be viewed as an intrusion into national sovereignty that fails to respect the local cultural context. If the international community "sorts out" the problem without building local leadership, it may inadvertently create a permanent state of dependency.


4. **"To what extent should governments prioritise the needs of the present over the future?"**
* **How it supports:** This case study illustrates the dilemma where short-term stability is a prerequisite for long-term progress. In Haiti, children cannot go to school (future) because it is unsafe outside due to active crossfire (present). It argues that governments must address immediate physiological and safety needs before education can resume.
* **Limitation/counterpoint:** Applying **Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs**, while safety is a "lower-order" priority that must be met first, focusing *exclusively* on the present can lead to a "lost generation." If education is sidelined indefinitely, the country loses the human capital necessary to solve future crises, effectively ensuring the cycle of conflict continues.



5. **"Is the elimination of global poverty a realistic aim?" (2009)**
* **How it supports:** The case study shows how poverty in Haiti is compounded by successive crises (the 2010 earthquake followed by skyrocketing gang violence in 2025). This suggests that the root causes of extreme poverty are perennial and systemic, leading to the exploitation of the next generation.
* **Limitation/counterpoint:** This can be evaluated through **Micro vs. Macro effectiveness**. While the macro elimination of poverty in Haiti may seem unrealistic given the geopolitical chaos, micro-interventions—such as "safe zones" for learning or targeted nutritional programs—can provide tangible exits from poverty for specific cohorts of children, even if the national economy remains fractured.
