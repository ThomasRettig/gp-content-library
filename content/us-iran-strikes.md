## Summary

US and Israel launched attacks on Iran on 28 February 2026, igniting conflict across the middle east. This led to Iran responding by attacking Israel and US-allied states in the gulf and closing the Strait of Hormuz. Hence, the oil prices began to soar. Recently, US had launched new strikes on Iran, targeting missile sites and boats, claiming it to be as “self defence” against the Iranian forces. With Iran yet responding to the US attacks, Iranian foreign minister spokesman stated that despite some progress being made in the talks to end the war, a deal “is not imminent”. However, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said that a deal was still possible with a lot of talking back and forth. He also stated that the Straits of Hormuz needs to be open as the closure of the Straits of Hormuz is illegal, unsustainable and unacceptable. The attack by the US is said to have attacked an area near Bandar Abbas, home of an Iranian naval base that sits on the Straits of Hormuz.

Additionally, a memorandum of understanding is being discussed over a 60-day ceasefire extension, the reopening of Strait of Hormuz and a plan for further negotiations over Iran’s nuclear programme. The US intelligence also believes that the Iran’s Supreme leader Mojtaba Khamenei being holed up in an undisclosed location id delaying the pace of talks with the US. At the start of the war, Iran is thought to have a lot of enriched uranium which could be used to create a nuclear bomb and Trump stated that it should be handed over to the US or destroyed in place.

1. **"Examine the claim that the world is too dependent on oil." (2005)**
	- **How it supports:** This case study illustrates the global economy's fragile dependency on oil; the closure of the Strait of Hormuz caused oil prices to "soar" globally, demonstrating how a localized regional conflict can trigger immediate worldwide economic instability.
	- **Limitation/counterpoint:** While the price surge highlights dependency, a **Symptom vs. Root Cause** analysis suggests that oil is merely the conduit for geopolitical leverage. The underlying issue is not just the resource itself, but the lack of "Energy Sovereignty" and diversified supply chains in importing nations, which makes them vulnerable to the "Geopolitical Realities" of transit chokepoints.


2. **"Is violence ever justified?" (2012)**
	- **How it supports:** This provides a basis for debating "justified" state action by comparing the US claim of "self-defence" for strikes on missile sites against the legalistic assertion that Iran’s closure of the Strait is "illegal" and "unacceptable."
	- **Limitation/counterpoint:** Using the **Normative vs. Descriptive** framework, one could argue that "justification" is often a matter of political framing rather than moral absolute. What the US labels as "self-defence" to maintain international law, Iran likely views as a "National Safety" necessity to counter external aggression, suggesting that justification is subjective and contingent on one's position in the power hierarchy.


3. **"Examine the extent to which expenditure on arms and the armed forces is justifiable in the modern world." (2014)**
	- **How it supports:** The case study links arms expenditure to "National Safety" needs, illustrating how Iran's investment in "missile sites" and "enriched uranium" serves as a deterrent or tool for regional influence, while the US utilizes its naval superiority to enforce its will.
	- **Limitation/counterpoint:** Applying the **Opportunity Cost** heuristic, such high expenditures on military capabilities can be seen as a failure of "Stewardship of National Resources." Money funneled into nuclear enrichment and naval bases represents a diversion of funds from "Physiological" and social developmental needs, potentially fueling a "Cycle of Violence" that offers no "Macro" return on human security.


4. **"‘The decisive use of force is the only practical means to resolve territorial conflicts.’ Discuss." (1995)**
	- **How it supports:** The US strikes on Iranian naval bases near the Strait of Hormuz test whether force "works" to reopen critical trade routes, weighing military action against the "talks" and "negotiations" cited by Marco Rubio.
	- **Limitation/counterpoint:** This can be critiqued through the **Effectiveness vs. Sustainability** framework. While the "decisive use of force" might provide "Short-term relief" by disabling missile sites, it fails to address the "Root Cause"—the nuclear dispute and ideological friction—leading to an "Unsustainable Peace" where conflict simply simmers until the next escalation.


5. **"Is it ever right for one country to become involved in the internal affairs of another?" (1997)**
	- **How it supports:** The US demand that Iran’s enriched uranium be "handed over or destroyed" represents a significant intervention in sovereign affairs, framed as a "moral duty" to prevent nuclear proliferation and maintain global safety.
	- **Limitation/counterpoint:** From the perspective of **Capability and Cultural Sovereignty**, such involvement is often perceived as an "imperial overreach" that ignores the "Internal Logic" of the target nation. Critics would argue that external intervention lacks the "Local Legitimacy" required to produce a stable outcome, often resulting in a "Power Vacuum" or increased radicalization rather than the intended resolution.
