## Summary

Dr. David Hasan, a Palestinian American neurosurgeon, runs schools in Gaza where children do math problems about soccer instead of martyrs. His network, Academies of Hope, currently serves 9,000 students across a free private school system built on a single editorial decision: strip the Palestinian Authority's standard textbooks of the sections that praise violent figures and replace them with lessons on tolerance and conflict resolution. This initiative is supported by concrete curriculum changes—such as swapping math problems about "martyrs" for ones about soccer, and replacing texts praising violent figures with profiles of educators—alongside strict staff vetting against U.S. and Israeli watchlists. Despite facing political pushback and threats from Hamas and the PA education ministry, the schools have secured significant humanitarian backing, including $1 million from chef José Andrés, providing vulnerable children with a desperately needed sanctuary for learning, daily meals, and psychological care.

## Personal reflection

The article frames Academies of Hope as a story about curriculum reform, but there is a deeper argument to be made: education in conflict zones is not just pedagogical, but political. That invites a degree of scrutiny when we evaluate the Palestinian Authority’s textbooks and Dr. Hasan’s project. I question the article’s binary framing that one is obviously correct and that the other is obviously pathological. I believe that both are acts of political inscription on children who did not choose any of this. Dr. Hasan works with Israeli and Jewish donors, vets staff against U.S. and Israeli watchlists, and teaches children to draw the Palestinian and Israeli flags side by side. That is a particular conception of peace grounded in the worldview of liberal coexistence, and the article does not question whether such a vision is appropriate, sufficient, or even actively useful to politically powerless children living in an active conflict zone.

Yet, the article meaningfully changed my understanding of education and the role of schools. I came in with a reasonably settled view that educational peace-building in active conflict zones is, at best, a long-term intervention with minimal immediate returns. However, this was predicated on my one-dimensional understanding of schools: that they are formal institutions of knowledge transfer. In reality, before a school teaches anything, it provides structure, warmth, food, other children, a desk, a pencil, a teacher who looks at you. Dr. Hasan’s schools certainly will not plant the seeds of long-term, large-scale reconciliation, but for displaced children scraping by at the bottom of Maslow’s hierarchy, schools like Dr. Hasan’s are doing something real and immediate.

## Essay question applicability

1. **“‘Countries experiencing conflict should be left to sort out their own problems.’ How far do you agree?” (2016)**
    - **How it supports:** This case study serves as a counter-argument to the idea of isolationism in conflict. It highlights how a **diaspora individual** (Palestinian American Dr. Hasan) and **international humanitarian figures** (chef José Andrés) have intervened to provide a "sanctuary for learning" and peace-building tools that the local warring factions have failed to provide.
    - **Limitation/counterpoint:** While external intervention like the "Academies of Hope" provides immediate relief, a counter-argument could be built using the Natural Factors heuristic, which includes geopolitical realities. One might argue that foreign-led initiatives, even humanitarian ones, can be perceived as an intrusion into a nation's "Physiological" need for national survival and sovereignty. Furthermore, applying the Capability heuristic, external actors may lack the deep Cultural understanding—the shared values and traditions—necessary to create a surmountable and lasting peace, potentially making their involvement a "Theory vs. Practice" failure where the ideal of help encounters the obstacle of local "political pushback".
2. **“‘Education should only be concerned with what is useful in life.’ Discuss.” (2013)**
    - **How it supports:** The "Academies of Hope" redefine "usefulness" in education. By stripping away militant rhetoric and replacing it with conflict resolution and tolerance, the curriculum argues that for children in Gaza, peace-building skills and psychological care are the most "useful" tools for survival and long-term stability, more so than standard political indoctrination.
    - **Limitation/counterpoint:** A limitation to the "peace-building" curriculum as the ultimate "useful" tool can be found in Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs. According to the sources, Physiological needs (food, water, shelter) and Safety needs (protection, stability) are the most important prerequisites that must be met before higher-level Cognitive or Transcendence needs can be pursued. In a zone of "widespread displacement," one could argue that an education focused on "tolerance" is less "useful" in the immediate term than vocational or survival-based training that addresses the urgent "needs of the present," such as securing basic life requirements.
3. **“To what extent can international aid be really effective?” (1996)**
    - **How it supports:** This is explicit an Effectiveness contention. The example illustrates aid that goes beyond financial support ($1 million) to include structural changes, such as curriculum reform and staff vetting. It tests whether private international aid can be effective even when facing "political pushback and threats" from local governing bodies like Hamas.
    - **Limitation/counterpoint:** This can be evaluated using the Symptom vs. Root Cause handle. While the $1 million in aid and the establishment of schools address the visible symptoms of the conflict (such as the lack of safe learning spaces and food insecurity), they may not tackle the underlying drivers or root causes of the war itself. The effectiveness of such aid is therefore Micro rather than Macro, as it improves the lives of 9,000 children but may not yield the "systemic social unity" required to end the broader "territorial conflict.
